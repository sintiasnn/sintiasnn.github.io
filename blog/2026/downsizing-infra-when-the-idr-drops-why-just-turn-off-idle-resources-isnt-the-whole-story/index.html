It started with a simple question from my manager:

“Why did our AWS cost get this bad last month?”

Not a demand to cut costs. Just an ask to analyze — figure out what actually happened. That question sent me down a rabbit hole, because the honest answer wasn’t one clean root cause. It was a mix of things, and the IDR was sitting right in the middle of it.

Here’s the thing: our cloud bills are in USD, but our budget is in IDR. When the exchange rate moves against us, the same infra that was “fine” last quarter suddenly eats a noticeably bigger chunk of the plan, even if nothing on our end actually changed.

That’s when the instruction that usually follows kicks in: downsize dev infra, cut costs.

Simple enough on paper. In practice? Not even close.

The advice everyone gives (and why it only half-works)

If you Google “cloud cost optimization,” you’ll get the same three tips every time: turn off idle resources, use spot instances, rightsize your instances. Solid advice — for the right workload, at the right time.

But here’s what nobody tells you: “idle” is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence.

In a dev environment where a feature ships almost every day, nothing is ever really idle. It’s active in the morning for testing, active in the afternoon for debugging, active in the evening for a demo to the PM. There’s no clean, safe window to just shut things down without stepping on someone’s work. And for a heavier service, one that takes real time to warm back up or reconnect to third-party integrations, spinning it back on costs more than money. It costs everyone’s time and momentum.

Spot instances have the same problem from a different angle. They’re cheap because the provider can yank the capacity back whenever they want, not when you want. Fine for a batch job that checkpoints itself. Not fine for a dev environment people are actively working in — an interruption there doesn’t save money, it just relocates the cost into lost focus and context-switching.

So the “textbook” moves don’t fail because they’re wrong. They fail because they assume a kind of stillness that a fast-moving project simply doesn’t have.

It’s not one infra, it’s many — and they don’t all deserve the same treatment

The other thing that gets lost in generic advice: a project isn’t one blob of infra. It’s a mix. A compute-heavy service that depends on third-party integrations, a lightweight internal API, a database holding actual state, a worker queue processing jobs. Each one has a completely different relationship with “downsizing.”

Squeeze the heavy service too hard and it just… can’t keep up with the third-party calls it depends on, or starts timing out. That’s not a cost saving, that’s an outage. Squeeze the lightweight API and probably nobody notices. Touch the database wrong and now you’re not optimizing cost, you’re risking data integrity.

Treating all of that with one blanket policy is how you end up cutting in the wrong place. The decision has to happen per component, not per project.

What actually changed how I approach this

My senior pushed me on something that reshaped how I think about all of this: decide from data, not from guessing.

It sounds obvious, but it’s easy to skip when you’re under pressure to cut costs now. The instinct is to eyeball it — “this instance feels oversized” — and just resize it. But without actually looking at CPU utilization, memory patterns, latency, and traffic over time, you’re gambling. And if it goes wrong, you don’t even have a clear reason why, which makes the next decision just as blind as the last one.

Data-driven downsizing means every cut is defensible. If someone asks “why did we drop this to X,” there’s a number behind the answer, not a shrug.

Infra needs a seat at the table before the project even starts

This is the part I’ve come to feel strongly about: cost problems that show up mid-project are often architecture problems that started at project kickoff.

If infra only gets brought in after the architecture is decided, we’re just there to provision what’s already been designed. By the time cost pressure hits, we’re stuck retrofitting savings onto a shape that was never built with cost or scalability in mind. Getting involved from the initial project declaration, helping shape the architecture around what this specific case actually needs, means fewer nasty surprises later. You’re not choosing between “keep the promised experience” and “cut the budget” as a last-minute panic. You designed for both from day one.

And it doesn’t stop at launch. Conditions keep shifting — new features, traffic spikes, changing load patterns — so monitoring has to be a loop, not a one-time check. Part of that loop is always having a plan B ready: if load spikes, what scales first; if we need to cut costs again, which component is safe to touch first, and which one absolutely isn’t.

Why this matters beyond the infra team

Here’s the thing that ties it all together for me: none of this lives in a vacuum. A PM isn’t just promising a client a finished feature — they’re promising an experience. How fast assets load, how responsive the app feels, how stable it is under load. All of that experience is sitting directly on top of the infra decisions we make.

So when cost pressure hits, downsizing isn’t just an internal “let’s save some money” call. It’s a call that can quietly break a promise someone already made to a client. That’s why the framework matters more than any single trick. Know which components tolerate being downsized. Know when, if ever, there’s a safe window to do it. And never lose sight of the experience riding on top of it all.

The IDR is going to keep doing what it does. What’s in our control is whether our decisions are based on data and a real understanding of the architecture — or just reacting to a number on a billing dashboard.
